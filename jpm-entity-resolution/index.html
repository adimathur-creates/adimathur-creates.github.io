Product proposal · Entity resolution · written for J.P. Morgan CIB Client 360

Same name, different company

A corporate bank holds one client under many IDs, and different clients under almost the same name. Matching them is only half the problem. The other half is product: explaining every merge, stopping the ones that are never safe, letting an analyst's fix survive the next rebuild, and deciding which source wins each attribute of the profile.

Aditya MathurBengaluruNovember 2026Code and 16 tests

Independent work. Not affiliated with or reviewed by J.P. Morgan, and no J.P. Morgan data. The 4,045 records per run are synthetic, built to look like five real source types: KYC onboarding, a credit system, a CRM, the GLEIF LEI registry and a commercial data vendor. Every noise rate, weight and threshold is a labelled assumption in the code. The numbers show trade-offs, not J.P. Morgan's results.

The short version

  • The problem. One legal entity can sit in a bank under several IDs and names, and onboarding, credit, compliance and reference data teams may each work from a different version [source]. Counterparty limits and BCBS 239 risk aggregation both depend on getting this right [source]. The Basel Committee still calls data lineage a challenging part of BCBS 239 in 2026 [source].
  • The costly error is the false merge. A missed match leaves a duplicate profile. A false merge puts two legal entities under one profile, so exposure, KYC status and ownership are wrong for both. Fuzzy name-and-address matching reached 92% recall but left 86 wrongly merged profiles per run, and merged 149 of 199 same-name twin companies.
  • The design. Evidence for and against every pair, each term explained. Three hard rules that no score can override. A hold for records that fit two companies equally. One review question per pair of clusters, not per pair of records. It cut wrongly merged profiles by 95% and duplicate profiles by 60% versus fuzzy matching, at 99.59% precision and 96.7% recall.
  • Overrides that stick. If an analyst's fix is stored against the golden-record ID, the next rebuild forgets it. Stored against source-record IDs and versions, it lasts until a record changes. Over six monthly rebuilds that meant 74% fewer review tasks and 0 reverted fixes instead of 10.
  • Vendors earn their money in the profile, not the match rate. Removing the commercial vendor barely moved match recall (97.1% → 96.8%). Profile accuracy fell from 92.3% to 81.7%, and industry codes from 86% to 55%.
  • Tested and parked: learning match weights from today's labels. The model needed 39% more human review than the hand-set rules at 99% precision, because today's labels are biased. Fix the labels first.

1Why this is the problem to solve

What the team owns

  • The Client 360 Product Associate role covers entity resolution, organisational profiles and third-party data integration. It also covers challenge-and-override workflows, explainability, attribute arbitration and lineage, measured with precision and recall.
  • Another J.P. Morgan posting describes entity resolution across internal and third-party sources that balances deterministic rules with ML-assisted matching, with every decision explainable, traceable and auditable [source].
  • Matching models are not the gap. J.P. Morgan has published neural entity linking work [source] and patented ML company matching with a feedback loop [source]. The open product questions are governance: what may merge automatically, what never may, and how a human correction persists.

Who feels a wrong profile

  • Credit risk: exposure to a counterparty is summed by legal entity. A false merge adds a subsidiary's exposure to its twin's; a duplicate splits it.
  • KYC and onboarding: a merged profile can show one entity as verified because its twin was.
  • Bankers: an incomplete view of a client group hides cross-sell and prospecting opportunities [source].
  • Reference data operations: analysts who validate and remediate Client 360 data every day [source]. Their time is the review budget.

Problem statement. Resolve records from internal systems and third-party vendors into one governed profile per legal entity. Keep false merges near zero, because they corrupt risk and compliance data. Keep duplicates low. Keep analyst work bounded, explain every decision, and make every human correction durable and auditable.

What each step bought

  • Scoring evidence against a match (distinguishing words, different legal forms, different postcodes) took wrong merges from 86 to 16, but recall fell to 84%.
  • Hard rules (different valid LEIs, different registration numbers, one record naming the other as its parent) halved wrong merges again, to 10.
  • The hold and the review band took wrong merges to 4.2 and recovered recall to 97%.

Why per-cluster review matters

A CRM record that resembles a company held by four sources creates four record-pair questions with one answer. Asking once per pair of clusters cut the queue from 1,339 to 385 questions a run, about 26 analyst hours at 4 minutes each. Pairs between clusters that a hard rule already separates never reach the queue.

Choose the thresholds

Auto-match is the score above which records merge with no human. The review floor is the score below which they are treated as different. Between them, an analyst decides.

Raising auto-match moves pairs from automatic to human. Lowering the floor catches more true matches but grows the queue.

4Overrides that survive the next rebuild

Profiles are rebuilt as sources refresh. In this simulation about 5% of records change each month, and downstream users catch half of the wrongly merged profiles they meet and raise a challenge. An analyst splits each one. The question is what happens to that split, and to every reviewer decision, next month.

Overrides stored against the golden-record ID (lost on rebuild)Overrides stored against source-record IDs and versions
Six monthsReview tasksAnalyst hoursUser challengesFixes undone by a rebuild
Overrides lost on rebuild2,3421561710
Overrides kept, re-verified when a record changes6094140

Two more seeds gave the same direction: review tasks 2,553 → 632 and 2,571 → 669; fixes undone 1 → 0 and 19 → 1.

Why the lost version is worse than it looks. Without memory, every reviewer decision is asked again, and a reviewer's 5% error rate is re-rolled each month, so some correct splits flip back. Users see the same wrong profile return after they reported it. That is how people stop trusting the golden source. With memory, a decision is re-asked only when one of its records changes. That's the re-verify rule.

The challenge-and-override workflow

User raises a challenge on a profile→Triage: merge, split or attribute fix→Maker proposes, with evidence→Checker approves→Stored as a constraint on source-record IDs + versions→Re-verified when a record changes
  • Split becomes a cannot-link between the records. Merge becomes a must-link. Attribute fix pins a value, with an expiry and the evidence behind it.
  • Every override records who, when, why, the evidence, and who approved it. That's the audit trail BCBS 239 lineage needs.
  • Overrides rank above model output and below a hard rule. An analyst can't merge two records with different valid LEIs without first fixing the LEI.

  • LEI registry: free, and worth 6.0% points plus 52 fewer review questions a run, because a shared valid LEI settles a pair without a person.

6Tested and parked: learning the weights

A model trained on the labels the process already produces did worse than the hand-set weights. I trained a logistic regression on 14,326 labels from one run: 373 reviewer decisions, plus ID-confirmed matches and hard-rule conflicts. I tested it on another run's 20,429 pairs that share no identifier, the hard part. To reach 99% precision it sent 5,151 pairs to review, against 3,695 for the hand weights. At 99.5% it was 5,470 against 4,597.

Why: ID-confirmed pairs teach the model that names don't matter, and hard-rule conflicts are mostly twins, so it learns the wrong lesson about similar names. Adding 800 randomly audited pairs still left it at 5,192. What I'd do: keep explainable rules for v1. Start a random audit sample now, because that's the only unbiased label set. Use ML first to rank the review queue, where a mistake costs minutes, not a wrong merge.

7Spec

Requirements (v1)

  1. Explainable decisions. Every match, non-match, hold and review decision stores its evidence terms, score, rule version and thresholds. The pair view above is the analyst screen.
  2. Hard rules beat scores. Different valid LEIs, different registration numbers in one country, or a parent-subsidiary link means never merge. LEIs are validated against ISO 17442 check digits first, so a typo is ignored instead of blocking a true match.
  3. Hold, don't guess. A record that strongly matches two clusters a hard rule keeps apart waits for review.
  4. Review by cluster. One question per pair of clusters in the grey zone, showing both clusters' records.
  5. Durable overrides. Stored on source-record IDs and versions, with maker-checker approval, and re-verified on material change.
  6. Attribute arbitration with lineage. Source precedence by attribute. Every value records its source, record, date and rule.

Rollout

  1. Shadow: run beside the current process for two refresh cycles. Compare every disagreement and label a random sample. That sample becomes the first unbiased truth set.
  2. Migrate existing overrides onto source-record keys before switching anything on, or the first rebuild undoes years of analyst work.
  3. Turn on by segment, starting where hard rules do most of the work (entities with LEIs), then the long tail without identifiers.
  4. Guardrails: stop auto-merging if the audited wrong-merge rate or the challenge rate crosses a set level.

Questions I'd bring to the team

  • How are overrides keyed today, and how many come back after a rebuild?
  • Which downstream consumer feels a false merge first: credit limits, KYC, or bankers' client views? That decides which segment to audit first.
  • How is a new vendor evaluated today: match rate, or what it adds to the profile?

8Method and limits

  • Synthetic data: 1,093 entities per run in about 380 corporate groups, with renames, moves, shared head offices, same-name twins and homonyms. Source quality rates are assumptions in src/data.js.
  • Blocking (same country and first name word, or any shared identifier) finds about 97% of true pairs. Most misses are renamed companies with no shared ID. In production this would need a former-names index.
  • Weights are hand-set log-odds, not fitted. That's deliberate for explainability (see section 6).
  • Reviewers are assumed right 95% of the time; users catch half of wrong merges each month. Neither has been measured.
  • Not modelled: corporate actions that create new legal entities, branches, funds, and multilingual names.
