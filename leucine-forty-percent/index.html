The Forty Percent Question

Leucine's job post asks: "How do we cut customer onboarding time by 40%?" This is my answer, worked through the way I would in the role: define the metric, find the real bottleneck, test the changes on a model, and turn the result into a spec, a sales line and a 90-day plan.

Aditya Mathur · October 2026 · prepared for Leucine's founder's office

Read this first. I do not work at Leucine and have no internal data. The model simulates 30 synthetic generic-pharma sites with 40 to 100 master batch records each. Its baseline is set to land close to Leucine's published rollout plan, which has batch records live in months 3 to 6. Every rate is my assumption and is listed below. The method is what I would bring: with real rollout logs, the same model can be rebuilt in a week.
1 · DEFINE THE PROBLEM

"Onboarding" needs one number first

Leucine's MES page says most sites go live in 8 to 12 weeks. That is the logbook phase. Its own rollout plan then puts batch records in months 3 to 6 and the AI layer in months 4 to 9. The value customers buy, such as Valent BioSciences cutting batch review from 20 days to 1, only arrives once batches run on electronic records. So I would measure onboarding like this:

Weeks from contract until 80% of a site's batch volume runs on electronic batch records. How do we cut that by 40% without loosening a single quality check?

Why 80% of volume, not every product

A generic site may hold 40 to 100 or more master batch records, but a few products make most batches. Counting products rewards finishing the long tail. Counting volume rewards what customers and auditors feel.

Why it matters to Leucine
  • Every week on paper is a week of batches that can miss a step.
  • Faster first-site value is the case for the next site, and customers like Cipla run 30.
  • Shorter rollouts free the configuration team for more sites.

3 · FIND THE BOTTLENECK

It is not Leucine's team

What if we only added capacityWeeks to 80% volume

Finding: four times more Leucine configurators saves about one week. Twice the customer review hours saves about four. The real limit is the order of work and the speed of customer decisions: reviewers spend their scarce hours on low-volume products while the products that make most batches wait in the queue.

4 · TEST THE CHANGES

Switch them on and off

Every run replays the same 30 sites with the same random draws, so differences come from the change alone.

Stress test. I reran the model with two other seeds, bigger and smaller sites, fewer and more reviewer hours, a lower first-time approval rate, and a smaller configuration team. The three no-engineering changes were 32% to 56% faster in every run. AI drafting on its own was never faster than today.

5 · RECOMMENDATION

Hit 40% without writing code, then compound

  1. Change the kickoff, this quarter

    Rank every master batch record by batches per month at kickoff and work in that order. Put a review sprint into the order form: named reviewers, a weekly block, and a 2-day sign-off. Start record configuration in week 2. In the model, these three changes alone take the median from 31 to 17 weeks, 45% faster.

  2. Build the block library next

    It adds another three weeks of speed and cuts Leucine's configuration hours by about 45% per site. That turns into margin and capacity for more sites. Generic sites share process families, so the library compounds with every customer.

  3. Ship AI drafting only with the library

    On its own, AI drafting moves rework onto the customer's reviewers, who are the bottleneck. With pre-reviewed blocks underneath, drafts start from approved parts, and the gain holds.

  4. Propose family-level parallel runs, never assume them

    It shortens the long tail the most (95% of products live much sooner). But it is the customer's QA decision. GAMP 5's second edition supports risk-based testing. FDA's final CSA guidance (September 2025) is written for device makers, so for drug makers it is supporting thinking, not a rule.

6 · SYSTEMATISE IT

A rollout control tower

The job post asks for every win to become a dashboard, an SOP or an automation. This is the dashboard that makes the playbook stick.

P0One row per master batch record: batches per month, complexity, family, owner, current step and days in that step.
P0Site view: share of batch volume live, and the next five records by volume and their blocker.
P0Review-sprint tracker: hours committed against hours used, and sign-offs past 2 days, sent weekly to the customer's site head.
P1Batch calendar link, so parallel runs are booked against the production schedule instead of waited for.
P1Portfolio view across sites, to compare rollouts and spot the reviewer who is holding three sites.
Metrics
  • Main: median weeks to 80% of batch volume live.
  • Leading: first-time approval rate and sign-off days.
  • Efficiency: Leucine configuration hours per site.
  • Guardrail: deviations in the first 30 days live, which must not rise.
How to prove it
  • Run the new kickoff on the next 4 to 6 site rollouts and compare with the last 6.
  • Read the result at 80% volume live, not at contract plus a fixed number of weeks.
7 · HAND IT TO SALES

The line sales can run with

"Commit a weekly review block during rollout, and most of your batch volume runs on electronic records in about four months."

Objection: "Our QA team is stretched." The total review hours stay the same. They come earlier, and they go to the products that make most of your batches first.

Objection: "Can we skip parallel runs?" Only where your QA agrees. We will propose one per process family, with the evidence.

8 · FIRST 90 DAYS

Shadow, then own

Days 1–30

Shadow two live rollouts and one sales cycle. Pull the record-level history of the last 6 sites. Rebuild this model on it and check whether reviewers really are the bottleneck.

Days 31–60

Write the new kickoff SOP and order-form clause with CS and sales. Build the control tower in Sheets first, then hand it to product.

Days 61–90

Run the new kickoff on the next rollouts. Report weeks to 80% volume to the founders, and write the block library business case with product.

9 · LIMITS

What this model does not know

  • Every rate is an assumption: record counts, hours, approval rates, reviewer time and batch frequency. The ranking held in every stress test; the sizes will change with real data.
  • Sites are treated one at a time. Customers rolling out several sites at once share reviewers, which makes the review sprint more valuable, not less.
  • Integration work (ERP, equipment) is folded into the site foundation. On some sites it is the true critical path.
