Repay on Payday
Lending Lab · experiment 01 · for Branch India

Repay on payday.

A rider is paid every Tuesday. A loan debit that lands on a Saturday hits an account that is already half spent. This page takes one lending idea from “what if” to a pilot that is ready to disburse: a test on old loans that could kill it before any code, the borrower flow, success and kill metrics fixed in advance, and the rule for the final call.

Aditya Mathur · November 2026 · Independent work on synthetic data. Not affiliated with Branch. Public figures are cited; everything else is labelled as an assumption.

The short version
  1. The bet: set the debit day from when the borrower gets paid, not from when the loan went out. Weekly earners repay weekly, the day after payout. Salaried borrowers repay the day after salary. Borrowers with no steady rhythm stay on today’s plan.
  2. Try to kill it for free first. Gate 0 uses only loans Branch has already made. If bounces don’t rise as the gap since the last income grows, the idea dies before anyone writes code.
  3. In the simulation (synthetic borrowers, … loans per arm): first-instalment bounce …, 30+ DPD at day 120 …, but … the debit attempts. That cost is real and is priced below.
  4. Fixed before launch: scale only if the bounce falls at least 3 points with a 95% interval above zero, and 30+ DPD is not worse by more than 1 point. Anything else at full sample is a kill, not an extension.
  5. First disbursed loan in four weeks, using a loan variant on Branch’s own NBFC book, a weekly UPI AutoPay mandate and the existing KFS flow. No new lending partner needed.
01 · Why this idea, and why first

Branch’s cost of credit is a collections problem as much as an underwriting one.

Branch India’s FY25 numbers, from its credit rating report: loans turn over fast, and a large share of income goes to write-offs and provisions. Anything that lifts on-time repayment without tightening approvals is worth a lot.

02 · Gate 0: try to kill it with old data

If timing doesn’t explain bounces in loans already made, stop here.

For each past loan, take the first due date and the last income credit before it. If debits far from an income day don’t bounce more than debits right after one, matching the date can’t help, and the idea is dead at zero build cost.

CSV columns: bounced plus either days_since_income or due_date and last_income_date. Read in your browser only; nothing is uploaded.

03 · The borrower flow

Same loan, same rate. Only the debit day moves.

Pick a borrower. The system reads 90 days of inflows, finds the pay rhythm, sizes the loan off a low month, and offers a plan that debits the day after money lands. The KFS numbers are computed from the actual cash-flow dates, so a weekly plan’s APR is checked, not assumed.

04 · The pilot, simulated

Run both plans on the same borrowers.

Each synthetic borrower has a fixed life: income days, spending habits, how often they set money aside on pay day, emergencies that eat savings, income shocks, and a small share who stop paying. Both arms live the same life, so the only difference is the debit day. UPI AutoPay is all or nothing, so a short wallet means a bounce.

The measurement trap. “Ever 30+ DPD” looks worse on weekly plans (…) because 13 instalments give 13 chances to be late once, even when the same money comes back. Pick that as the guardrail and you kill a good idea. The guardrail here is a snapshot on one date for both arms: share of loans 30+ days past due at day 120.

05 · Pre-registered spec

Success, kill and guardrails, written before the first loan.

ItemDecision
HypothesisDebiting the day after money lands cuts first-instalment bounce for borrowers with a steady pay rhythm, without raising late defaults.
PopulationNew loans where the rhythm detector reads weekly, daily or monthly. Irregular earners are excluded and stay on today’s plan.
ArmsControl: today’s due date. Matched: weekly the day after payout (weekly earners), weekly on a fixed day (daily earners), monthly the day after salary (salaried). Same amount, rate, fee and tenure.
Randomisation50/50 at the loan, by a hash of the loan ID, so assignment is reproducible and can’t be picked by anyone.
Primary metricFirst-instalment bounce: the first instalment not collected by the due date plus the retry days.
Guardrails30+ DPD snapshot at day 120 not worse by more than 1 point. Complaints per 1,000 loans and mandate cancellations tracked weekly.

Early stopOnly for harm: kill the day the 30+ DPD gap is above the guardrail with confidence. No early scale on a good week.

07 · Path to the first disbursed loan

Four weeks, with sign-offs first.

Week 1
Confirm today’s due-date logic and kill it on paper. Run Gate 0 on last quarter’s loans. Get Credit and Compliance sign-off on a repayment-schedule variant (same price) on Branch’s own NBFC book, and on the weekly KFS. Confirm the PSP supports weekly UPI AutoPay with pre-debit notices.
Week 2
Detector and schedules. Run the rhythm detector on the inflow data Branch already uses for underwriting and back-test its reads. Add weekly schedules and the overdue sweep to the loan system. Make sure payments go to the oldest dues first.
Week 3
Borrower flow behind a flag. Plan picker, KFS, mandate set-up, pre-debit SMS. Dogfood with staff loans. Set up dashboards for the primary metric and guardrails before go-live.
Week 4
First 50 loans, then ramp. Watch mandate success and the notice pipeline daily. Ramp to the planned sample once 50 loans go through clean.

08 · What would kill it

Six ways this fails, and how we’d know early.

  1. Branch already sets due dates near pay days. Then the salaried win is already banked, and the real test is weekly plans for weekly and daily earners. Check this in week 1.
  2. Debit costs. Weekly plans mean about three times the attempts. If the PSP price per attempt is far above the assumption, the economics go negative. The calculator above shows the break-even.
  3. Mandate fatigue. More debits may lead to more cancelled mandates. This is tracked weekly as a guardrail, and the plan has a monthly fallback.
  4. The KFS APR goes up on weekly plans. Same monthly rate and fee, lower rupee cost, but the fee is spread over a shorter average balance, so the all-in APR rises (see the borrower flow). Compliance must sign off on this, or the weekly fee is cut to hold APR flat. Decide this in week 1, not after launch.
  5. The detector misreads. A wrong payday is worse than today’s random date. Mitigation: only enter borrowers whose read is clear, and log the confidence on every read.
  6. The effect is selection, not timing. Randomising at the loan stops this: both arms have the same mix of people.
Rules this design respects (RBI Digital Lending Directions, 2025, and UPI AutoPay)

The KFS shows the all-in APR before signing. The cooling-off window lets a borrower exit by paying principal plus proportionate APR, with no penalty; the length is board-set, at least one day. No credit-limit increase without an explicit request. Every weekly debit gets a pre-debit notice at least 24 hours ahead. Debits up to ₹15,000 run without extra authentication; above that, the borrower approves each one, which is why the overdue sweep is capped at ₹15,000.

A changed repayment schedule may need Branch’s lending partners to approve if those loans sit on their books, which is why the pilot runs on Branch’s own book.

Why I built this. Lending Lab’s job is to take a “what if” to the first disbursed loan, and to kill ideas when the data says stop. So this page starts with a test that can kill the idea, fixes the kill rule before launch, and shows the one metric choice that would have killed a good idea by mistake.
