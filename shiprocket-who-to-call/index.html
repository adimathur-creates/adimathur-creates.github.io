Who should TwentyTwo call? Deciding which cash-on-delivery orders deserve a confirmation call

TwentyTwo lists COD confirmation as its commerce use case. Every voice vendor sells it the same way: call every COD order, report the share that confirmed. This page argues that the confirmation rate ranks the work backwards, sets out a call policy based on what each call is worth, designs the call itself, and says how I would prove it on real sellers.

The short version

  • Problem: a seller judges a COD call by delivered orders and RTO cost. A vendor reports confirmations. If TwentyTwo calls every COD order, the seller pays for calls that change nothing and loses a few buyers who would have accepted delivery.
  • Finding (default assumptions): on a confirmation-rate dashboard, the low-risk band looks best (97% confirmed) while each call there loses about Rs 3. The high-risk band looks worst (72%) and earns about Rs 57 per call.
  • Policy: call an order only when its predicted RTO risk is above a break-even point worked out from two numbers the seller knows: margin per order and cost of an RTO. At defaults that point is about 10%. Skipping the low band halves call spend, nearly halves genuine buyers lost, and nets about 10% more than calling everyone.
  • Proof: a holdout inside each risk band, measured on delivered COD orders net of call spend. A holdout of about 365 orders per arm in the medium band detects the expected effect. The holdout stays on after launch, at 10%, so the seller report is a measurement, not a claim.

1. The problem, stated narrowly

COD still decides whether an Indian D2C order makes money. Published benchmarks put COD RTO far above prepaid: ClickPost's FY2026 D2C network saw 37.5% of COD orders end as RTO against 5.3% for prepaid, and Meesho's FY25 prospectus reported 22.3% vs 2.7%. Shiprocket's own blog puts COD RTO at 25 to 35%.

A pre-dispatch call can help in three ways: a buyer who never meant to accept cancels before the parcel ships, an address gets fixed, or a doubtful buyer is reassured or moves to prepaid. It can also hurt in one way that nobody's dashboard shows: a buyer who would have accepted the parcel hears a robot asking "do you still want this?" and says no.

Problem statement: for a seller on Shiprocket, decide which COD orders TwentyTwo should call, what the call should do, and how to show the seller the value in delivered orders and rupees, so that the product pays for itself per seller rather than per call.

Out of scope: NDR calls after a failed delivery attempt (a different moment with different economics), COD-to-prepaid at checkout, and pricing TwentyTwo itself.

2. Why the confirmation rate ranks the work backwards

A confirmation-rate dashboard counts everyone who didn't cancel. It mixes up three different things: would-be RTOs that the call caught, genuine buyers it lost, and calls that changed nothing. The band with the fewest RTOs has the most "confirmations" and the least to gain.

Default assumptions, per 100 COD orders in each band. Net value counts RTO cost avoided, margin from deliveries the call saved, margin lost on genuine buyers who cancelled, and call spend.

Risk bands and call effects

Catch rate: of would-be RTOs who answer, the share who cancel before dispatch. Save rate: of the remaining would-be RTOs who answer, the share the call turns into a delivery. Genuine cancel: of buyers who would have accepted delivery and answer, the share the call talks out of the order.

4. The decision rule

The value of calling one order rises in a straight line with its RTO risk, so there is a single risk level where a call breaks even. For each seller, TwentyTwo works it out from two numbers the seller already knows (margin per order and what an RTO costs them) plus the call effects the pilot measures. It then calls only orders above that level.

Where the risk score comes from

This is the part a standalone voice vendor can't copy. Shiprocket already has the inputs: the Sense RTO prediction and address-quality APIs, Checkout's check of the buyer's last successful delivery address, pincode and courier performance from Radar, and the buyer's history across sellers on the network (a phone number that refused three COD parcels last month elsewhere). TwentyTwo's commerce advantage is deciding who to call. The voice is the cheaper part.

The bands here are the simplest version. Once pilot data exists, the same rule applies per order to the Sense score itself, and the call effects can be estimated by segment (first-time buyer, category, order value).

5. The call itself

A COD confirmation call has one job: find out whether this person will accept this parcel, and fix what would stop them. These are the steps and the rules that govern each. The lines are Hinglish because that's how most of these buyers answer.

  • 1 · Open, say it's AI, check it's the buyer "Namaste, main {brand} ki AI assistant bol rahi hoon. Kya meri baat {name} se ho rahi hai?" If someone else answers, don't name the product or the amount. COD parcels are often gifts. Ask when the buyer can be reached and end the call.
  • 2 · Intent, asked so the answer has to carry information "Aapka order, {product}, {amount} rupaye cash on delivery, kal ya parson tak aayega. Aap kis time ghar par milenge?" "Haan" on its own is not a confirmation. A time, a person who will collect it, or a landmark is. A bare yes is logged as confirmed_vague, the order still ships, and a WhatsApp message with the delivery window follows.
  • 3 · Address check (only when Sense flags the address) "Address mein landmark nahi hai. Ghar ke paas koi landmark bata sakte hain?" The correction goes through the address API and is read back before it's saved.
  • 4 · Prepaid offer: once, after intent, only if the seller has switched it on "Abhi UPI se pay karein toh {offer} off milega. Link WhatsApp par bhej doon?" Asked once. A no ends the topic. The link goes on WhatsApp; no payment details are taken on the call.
  • 5 · Cancel only on an explicit request "Theek hai, kya main order cancel kar doon?" Silence, confusion or "abhi nahi pata" is never a cancellation. A cancel is confirmed once, then written to the order system with the order ID as the idempotency key. If that write times out, the agent checks the order's status before saying anything, and never retries blindly.
  • 6 · Unreachable Two attempts inside the calling window, then WhatsApp. What happens to an unconfirmed order is the seller's setting, per band: default ship for low and medium risk, hold up to 24 hours for high risk.

Outcomes written back: confirmed_detailconfirmed_vagueaddress_fixedprepaid_link_sentcancelled_explicitwrong_personunreachable. Only cancelled_explicit changes the order. Everything else is information.

6. What must pass before a real buyer is called

An eval set of about 40 scripted calls across eight types: clear yes, bare "haan", gift recipient answering, wrong number, address fix, prepaid accepted, explicit cancel, and a buyer who changes their mind mid-call. Each runs in Hindi, English and mixed speech, with interruptions.

Blocks launch any cancellation without an explicit request · product or amount told to someone who isn't the buyer · the prepaid offer repeated after a no · an order-system write that can't be matched to its order ID.

Tracked, doesn't block share of vague confirmations, call length, early hang-ups, language switches handled.

7. The pilot

The catch, save and genuine-cancel rates are the numbers this whole policy rests on, and no one has them for Shiprocket sellers yet. You also can't see a lost genuine buyer directly: you only see a cancellation. So the pilot doesn't try to measure them one by one. It measures what the seller cares about against a holdout.

  • Design: 5 to 10 consenting sellers. Inside each seller, eligible COD orders are randomly assigned to a call or to no call, separately within each risk band, including the low band. Calling the low band in the pilot is the only way to learn whether skipping it is right.
  • Primary metric: net value per 100 COD orders placed = delivered orders × margin − RTOs × RTO cost − call spend, compared with the holdout in the same band.
  • Ignored as a target: confirmation rate. It's logged, never optimised.
  • Guardrails: buyer complaints, seller opt-outs, and buyers who cancel on a call and reorder the same item within 14 days (the closest visible sign of a genuine buyer lost).
  • Size:
  • Decision: per band, call if the lift in net value is positive with a lower bound above zero, extend if it's unclear, stop calling the band if it's negative. Any blocking failure from section 6 pauses calls immediately.

8. What this changes in TwentyTwo

  • Default setting: "Call where it pays", not "call every COD order". The seller enters margin and RTO cost once; TwentyTwo sets the threshold and shows it.
  • Seller report: delivered COD orders and rupees against the holdout, by band. Not "orders confirmed". This is also TwentyTwo's best sales asset: a number the seller can check.
  • Always-on holdout: 10% of eligible orders are never called. It costs a little value and keeps every claim measurable as categories, seasons and buyer behaviour change.
  • Pricing, later: per-call pricing is honest while the effects are uncertain. Pricing per saved RTO only makes sense once the holdout numbers are stable.

9. What I'd want to know from inside Shiprocket

  • How well calibrated is the Sense RTO score by band? The rule needs a probability, not just a ranking.
  • What do sellers do today with unconfirmed COD orders: ship, hold or cancel? That default drives most of the value.
  • Is there existing call or IVR data (the NDR voice bot) that shows cancellation rates by risk band? It would set better priors than mine.
