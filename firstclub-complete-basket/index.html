The Complete Basket

How much of each fresh item should go to each clubhouse every morning, so a customer's whole basket arrives without premium produce being thrown away. A working replenishment simulator, the policy I would ship, and how I would test it.

Aditya Mathur · October 2026 · prepared for the FirstClub supply chain product team

Read this first. I do not work at FirstClub and have no internal data. The model runs on synthetic orders for 24 clubhouses (21 in Bengaluru, 3 in Hyderabad, matching public counts) and 78 fresh items. All demand, shelf life, margin and cost figures are my assumptions and are listed on this page. The method is what transfers: with real order and stock logs, the same simulator can be rerun in a week.
1 · THE PROBLEM

Quality is promised per basket, not per item

Every morning, how much of each fresh item should the hub send to each clubhouse, so that a customer's basket arrives complete while waste on short-life, premium produce stays low, and how should that change as FirstClub adds clubhouses, cities and categories?

The founder's promise is "the right quality selection, consistently delivered every single time". Customers buy about ten items per order. A basket is only as good as its weakest item: if one of them is missing, the order fails the promise even when the other nine are perfect. Three things make this harder for FirstClub than for a mass-market dark store.

  • Low stock per store by design. Clubhouses keep thin inventory to protect freshness, so there is little buffer when demand jumps.
  • Premium, short-life items lead. Top sellers include avocados, persimmons and Modi apples, not onions and potatoes. Each unit thrown away costs more.
  • Quality checks use up shelf life. Stock is checked at the warehouse, at the clubhouse and before dispatch. Every hour a lot waits for release is an hour of life lost on the shelf.

4 · THE TRADE-OFF

More complete baskets usually cost more waste. It does not have to.

Each line shows one way of setting stock, from cautious to generous. Up is more complete baskets. Left is less money thrown away. The best policies sit top-left.

One target for every item Target priced per item Priced, plus same-morning quality release ● Today (assumed)

At about 97% complete baskets, one target for every item throws away about Rs 2,570 per clubhouse per day. Pricing the target per item cuts that to about Rs 1,660. Adding same-morning quality release brings it to about Rs 510, and items reach customers with more life left.

What "priced per item" means: for each item at each clubhouse, the target is set by comparing two costs. One is the cost of running out: the lost margin plus a penalty for breaking the basket. The other is the cost of having too much: the unit's cost times the chance it expires before it sells. Milk at a busy clubhouse gets a high target, because leftovers sell tomorrow. A Rs 349 tray of raspberries at a small clubhouse gets a lower one.

5 · TEST THE LEVERS

Switch the changes on and off

Every run replays the same customers, so differences come from the change alone. Margin is fresh gross margin after waste and after any extra trip or staff cost.

Margin change per clubhouse per day, each change added on its own to today

Stress test. I reran the model with two other random seeds, more accurate launch estimates, a floor of 1 unit instead of 3, 20% fewer orders, and a cheaper trip. Priced targets beat today in every run, adding Rs 750 to 1,400 per clubhouse per day. Same-morning release added Rs 700 to 810 on top in every run. The second drop lost money in every run except when a trip cost Rs 200, where it added about Rs 60.

6 · RECOMMENDATION

What I would ship, in order

  1. Daily, priced targets per item and clubhouse

    The biggest single gain, and it is software only. It needs one data fix first: the app must log every item a customer wanted but could not add, or the forecast learns from a number that hides the problem. In the model, complete baskets rise from about 84% to about 95% while waste falls by about a quarter.

  2. Same-morning quality release, as a hub pilot

    Not fewer tests. The same tests, done overnight. It needs a supplier arrival cut-off and a night QC shift, so it is an operations change that product has to make visible. It gives customers fresher produce, which is the brand.

  3. New clubhouse launch playbook

    Hyderabad shows the cost of copying Bengaluru levels: in the model only about 64% of baskets arrive complete. Start new clubhouses on the daily forecast from day one, seeded from the three most similar existing clubhouses, and review daily for the first two weeks.

  4. Hold the second drop

    At Rs 400 a trip, the waste it saves does not cover the trip. Revisit it for bakery only, or once trips can be shared with another flow such as new categories or returns.

7 · SPEC FOR THE FIRST CHANGE

Daily priced replenishment

Users
  • Hub planner: reviews and releases the overnight allocation.
  • Clubhouse lead: receives stock and flags damage or mismatch.
  • Category manager: sets the basket penalty and protects launch items.
User story

As a hub planner, I want each clubhouse's fresh allocation to be calculated every night from real demand and from what running out or wasting each item costs, so I spend my time on the exceptions, not on typing in numbers.

P0Log lost demand: every add-to-cart blocked by stock, every "notify me" tap, and every substitution, by item, clubhouse and hour.
P0Nightly forecast per item and clubhouse, adjusted for weekday, with a warm start for new items and clubhouses from similar ones.
P0Target per item and clubhouse from the two costs, using margin, landed cost, shelf life left after release, and the basket penalty set by category.
P0Planner view: allocation by clubhouse, items flagged when the change from yesterday is large, override with a reason.
P1Event uplifts (festivals, weather, launches) entered by category managers, applied to the forecast and tracked for accuracy.
P1Daily report: complete-basket rate, waste by item and clubhouse, and which items broke baskets.
Metrics
  • North star: share of orders delivered with every item.
  • Waste: fresh write-offs as % of fresh cost of goods.
  • Quality: average shelf life left when the item reaches the customer.
  • Guardrails: substitution rate, refund rate for quality, planner overrides per day.
How to test it
  • Split Bengaluru clubhouses into matched pairs by size and area. One of each pair gets the new allocation, the other keeps today's levels.
  • Run for 4 weeks, so every item cycles through its shelf life several times.
  • Ship if complete baskets rise and waste does not, with no rise in quality refunds.
Risks
  • Planners distrust the numbers and override everything. Mitigation: show why each number changed, and track override accuracy.
  • A new item has no history. Mitigation: seed from similar items and keep a launch floor for its first two weeks.
Dependencies
  • App events for lost demand.
  • Landed cost and shelf life per lot in the warehouse system.
  • Sign-off from category and hub operations on the basket penalty.

9 · FIRST 90 DAYS

How I would run this

Days 1–30

Walk the hub at night and three clubhouses at peak. Pull order, stock and write-off logs. Rebuild this model on real data and agree the complete-basket metric with category and operations.

Days 31–60

Ship lost-demand logging. Build the nightly forecast and priced targets behind a planner review screen. Pick matched clubhouse pairs and start the test.

Days 61–90

Read out the test. Scale to all Bengaluru clubhouses if it passes. Start the night-QC pilot with the hub team, and use the forecast for the next Hyderabad openings.

10 · LIMITS AND OPEN QUESTIONS

What this model does not know

  • Every number is an assumption, including demand, shelf life, margins, trip and shift costs. The ranking of the changes held in every stress test, but the size of each gain will change with real data.
  • The hub is assumed to always have stock. Supplier shortfalls and fill rates are not modelled.
  • Each item is sold as one unit and there are no substitutes. Real customers sometimes swap, which softens a stock-out.
Questions I would bring to the team
  • How are clubhouse stock levels set today, and how often do planners override them?
  • Does the app log items customers wanted but could not add?
  • How long does a lot wait between arriving at the hub and being released?
  • How do substitutions and refunds for quality show up in the data?
