A forward-deployed firm wins the deal on a metric the client agrees to. It keeps the client by proving that metric month after month, and by catching the agent going quietly wrong before the client does. This is the function I would build first: deployment assurance.

The four deployments are illustrative, shaped like workflows a used-car marketplace publicly says it runs on agents. Every volume, error rate and rupee figure is a labelled assumption in the code. The numbers show trade-offs, not anyone's results.

What I would own

Deployment assurance: the number, kept true. For every live deployment, one person owns three things after go-live:

  • A value number the client signs: in rupees, measured against a holdout, with an error bar.
  • An error rate measured by audits, with drift caught in days, not when the client complains.
  • A monthly value statement that becomes the renewal case and, with permission, the next sales case study.

The number that says it worked: by month three, every live deployment has a signed value statement, and the client finds zero incidents before we do.

The short version

  • Why this, why now. Deployment Inc starts each engagement with a specific workflow and an agreed metric, and stays until the workflow runs reliably at scale [source]. Its co-founder puts it this way: the product is not the agent, it's the changed operation [source]. Both promises need someone measuring after launch.
  • Benchmarks stop at launch. ReliabilityBench, the CTO's own work, tests agents before launch, and notes that production adds users who phrase things differently, APIs that fail and data formats that change [source]. In production there's no answer key, so you need audits.
  • Catching drift. I simulated four months of three deployments, each hit by one loud drift (confidence drops) and one silent drift (confidence doesn't). Watching confidence caught every loud drift within a day but missed all 30 silent ones. Waiting for complaints caught everything, after about a week, with 6 false alarms per run.
  • The design that worked: risk-stratified audits plus a confidence watch, with the same 100 audits a day allocated by money at risk. It caught all 60 drifts and cut drift losses by 54% versus waiting for complaints.
  • Proving value. For a lead re-engagement agent, crediting every returning lead claimed ₹87.3L/month, and a before/after comparison claimed ₹35.0L. The true lift was ₹25.6L. A 5% holdout read ₹27.8L ± ₹4.7L, for a cost of ₹1.4L a month.

1Why this is the function to build first

What the firm promises

  • Forward-deployed engineers start with one workflow and an agreed metric, deploy on live operations, and stay until it works reliably at scale [source].
  • It grew out of Cars24, where more than 45 agents run across support, sales, financing, inspections, payments and internal operations [source]. Cars24 is also its first customer [source].
  • Target sectors include BFSI, healthcare, manufacturing and logistics [source]. There, a wrong approval costs real money and an unproven saving doesn't renew.

What the public story leaves out

  • OpenAI's Cars24 case study reports outcomes: over a million conversation minutes a month, 12% of lost seller leads recovered, 50% higher support resolution [source]. It doesn't describe how agents are evaluated or monitored after launch.
  • Pre-launch reliability now has a vocabulary: consistency across runs, robustness to rephrasing, tolerance of tool failures [source]. Post-launch assurance doesn't. As a firm goes from 3 deployments to 30, it can't run on FDE memory.
  • Every claimed result is a sales asset. A result measured against a holdout survives a client's CFO.
  • The team that built 45+ agents at Cars24 almost certainly does much of this already. My first job would be to learn how, then make it the same for every client.

Problem statement. After go-live, (1) catch an agent's drift in days, including drift its own confidence can't see, with a fixed review budget; and (2) report each deployment's value in rupees, measured against what would have happened anyway, with an honest error bar.

2Catching drift before the client does

Confidence is half the answer

It sees loud drift within a day, and silent drift never. An agent that's confidently wrong looks healthy on every dashboard built from its own scores.

Random audits are too thin

At 1% of volume, random audits were slower than complaints (9 vs 7 days on silent drift). Most audited cases are easy ones that were never going to fail.

Put audits where the risk is

Send 60% of audits to low-confidence and novel-looking cases, and run a separate alarm on each group. Then give each deployment audits by money at risk, not volume.

3Proving the value is real

A lead re-engagement agent calls leads that dropped out. Some of those leads would have come back anyway, more of them in festive season, and more again after a price change on day 70. All three effects are in the simulation, alongside a true agent lift of 2 points.

  • Crediting every return claims 3.4x the true value. A "share of lost leads recovered" metric tells you activity, not impact.
  • Before/after overstates by 36% here, because festive season and the price change land in the "after" window.
  • A holdout (leads the agent randomly doesn't call) gets the true value within its error bar.

5Runbook

Before go-live

  1. Agree the metric and the holdout with the client owner in writing.
  2. Set the acceptance error rate from a labelled sample, and keep that sample as the regression set.
  3. Define "risky" for this workflow: low confidence, never-seen templates or callers, high rupee value.
  4. Size the audit budget by money at risk, and staff it (client team, our team or a vendor).

After go-live

  1. Daily: stratified audits, a separate alarm per group, and a confidence watch.
  2. On an alarm: the FDE triages within a day, finds the segment, then fixes, rolls back or routes those cases to people.
  3. Tell the client about every incident before it shows up downstream.
  4. Monthly: value statement, error with error bar, incidents, an expand-or-hold call.

My first 30 days

Week 1Sit with the FDEs on the Cars24 deployments. Write down what "working" means for each, and how anyone would know it stopped.
Week 2Stand up audits and a holdout on one live deployment. Ship the first value statement draft to the founders.
Week 3Turn it into a template every new engagement starts with: metric, holdout, audit plan, acceptance gate.
Week 4First signed value statement. Turn the best number into a one-page case study for the sales team.

Two more things I'd raise in month one

  • A BFSI deployment pack. Banks and NBFCs will ask about data handling, audit trails and model-risk documentation before the first workflow is chosen. I've worked in AML at Stripe; I'd write the pack once, so every FDE doesn't answer it from scratch.
  • Deal qualification from the assurance side. If a workflow has no measurable outcome, or no way to audit it, it will be hard to renew. Better to know that before signing.

6Method and limits

  • Daily, group-level simulation (src/sim.js): each deployment-day is split into low-confidence or novel cases versus the rest, with binomial errors and audits. CUSUM alarms test whether a group's error rate has doubled.
  • Assumed: drift hits 15% of cases at 25% error; a novelty flag spots 60% of drifted cases; clients notice 8–30% of errors; complaints are reported 3 days late. Change any of these in ASSUME.
  • Audits are assumed correct. In practice reviewers disagree, so you'd double-audit a slice to measure that.
  • The value ledger (src/ledger.js) assumes a clean randomised holdout. In real deployments, holdout leakage and client pushback ("why aren't you calling everyone?") are the hard part.
  • Not modelled: model or prompt updates by the FDE team (each one should re-run the regression set), cost of reviewer time, and multi-step agent failures.
