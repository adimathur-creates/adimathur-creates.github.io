Product case · Prior authorization · US healthcare · November 2026

Complete on first submit

Health plans have promised real-time prior authorization by 2027. That promise only counts requests that arrive complete. This is the first program increment I would run as an Associate Technical Product Manager to get there for one service, lumbar spine MRI, before the 1 January 2027 Prior Authorization API deadline.

By Aditya MathurLinkedInCode, backlog files and tests

Independent work. I don't work at Optum or UnitedHealthcare, and this is not their product, plan or policy. Facts come from public sources listed at the end. The clinical rules are illustrative, based on the public ACR Appropriateness Criteria, not any health plan's medical policy. Patients and the 1,000-request sample are synthetic.

In one minute

  • The deadline. From 1 January 2027, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP and federal-exchange plans must run a FHIR Prior Authorization API that lets providers check requirements, submit, and track decisions.
  • The promise. UnitedHealthcare and about 50 other insurers pledged that at least 80% of electronic prior-auth approvals will be answered in real time by 2027. The pledge only covers requests sent electronically with all the clinical documentation needed.
  • The gap. Completeness is the bottleneck. In my synthetic sample, pre-filling from the chart alone completed 1.8% of requests. Asking for only the missing items before submit raised that to 55.5%.
  • Two findings that shaped the backlog. "No red flags" can never be pre-filled: a blank chart isn't a clinician saying "none", so it blocked 96.2% of incomplete requests. After that fix, the 30-day office-note rule was the top remaining blocker (53.3%). So one story adds a one-tap confirmation, and one spike challenges the rule.
  • What is here. Personas, a journey map, today-vs-target process flows, a working prototype, 17 user stories with Given/When/Then criteria, WSJF priorities, a sprint plan with a cut line for 1 January, OKRs, risks, and a backlog you can import into Rally or GitHub.

1The problem, and why it is this team's problem now

  • Rules with dates. The CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization rule (CMS-0057-F) already requires decisions within 72 hours for urgent and 7 calendar days for standard requests, and a specific reason for every denial. Payers posted their first public prior-auth metrics on 31 March 2026. The FHIR API deadline, 1 January 2027, is the one most teams are least ready for.
  • A promise with a condition. AHIP is clear that the 80% target applies only when requests arrive electronically with all needed documentation. It does not mean 80% of all requests are approved instantly.
  • Most requests aren't even electronic. The 2025 CAQH Index puts electronic prior authorization at 40%, up from 31% in 2023.
  • Incomplete requests cost days. When documentation is missing, plans send a letter listing what is needed, typically within 3 business days, and the clock restarts on the provider's side. In Medicare Advantage, most appealed denials (81.7% in 2023) were overturned. KFF notes this may mean the first request lacked documentation.
  • Most requests already get approved. UnitedHealthcare says prior auth applies to about 2% of its medical services, and about 92% of requests are approved, in under 24 hours on average. So the friction sits in the incomplete requests, not in the typical one.

Where Optum already plays, and where this backlog fits

In February 2026 Optum launched two AI products for this exact problem. Digital Auth Complete (with Humata Health) sits in the EHR: it works out when prior auth is needed, gathers documentation and submits. InterQual Auth Accelerator helps payer reviewers check documentation against InterQual criteria, and Optum says it does not and will not automate denials. This case doesn't compete with either. It shows how I would break one slice of that work, for one service, into a backlog an engineering team can ship by 1 January, using the open HL7 Da Vinci standards (CRD to check coverage, DTR to pre-fill documentation, PAS to submit).

5Evidence for the order of work


I simulated 1,000 lumbar MRI requests with realistic gaps in chart data, and ran them through the same rules in two ways. Every rate is an assumption, written down in src/sample.js; what carries over is the method.




Pre-fill only
1.8% complete on first submit. Everything else would be pended.


Pre-fill, then ask before submit
55.5% complete: 31.4% approved in real time, 24.1% sent to a clinician, 44.5% still missing something. The rules auto-approved 0 requests whose true story did not meet them.

  • Put the one-tap red-flag confirmation (S3.2) and "never assume none" (S2.3) in the first two sprints. They unlock most of the gain for very little work.
    • 

  • Take the recent-note rule back to the business as a question, not a given (spike S7.1). If structured answers already prove the case, does a reviewer still need the note? That is the "re-evaluate requirements" part of the job.

Why completeness, not the pledge number, is the metric to manage


The pledge measures real-time answers among requests that are already complete and electronic. Patients feel the whole funnel. Move the sliders.














6Goals


Prior auth for lumbar MRI is decided at the speed of care
  • First-pass complete rate up 20 points from the week-1 baseline by 31 Mar 2027
  • At least 80% of complete electronic requests that meet the rules are approved in real time (the industry pledge definition)
  • Median days lost to "send more information" loops down 50% for the pilot service
Compliant and auditable from 1 Jan 2027
  • Prior Authorization API live for the pilot service on 1 Jan 2027
  • 100% of pended or denied requests carry a specific reason and what to send
  • Zero decisions past 72 hours (urgent) or 7 calendar days (standard)


Guardrails
  • No automated denials: anything that does not clearly meet the rules goes to a clinician
  • Clinician audit of a 5% sample of auto-approvals agrees at least 98% of the time
  • Questions asked per request do not go up (pre-fill must remove work, not add it)

Definition of Ready
  • Written as a user story with a named persona
  • Acceptance criteria in Given / When / Then
  • Estimated by the team
  • Dependencies named and either cleared or scheduled
  • Test data exists (synthetic patients)
  • Clinical or policy sign-off where a rule is involved


Definition of Done
  • All acceptance criteria pass in a test environment
  • Automated tests added and green
  • Peer review done; no new security findings
  • Audit logging in place for any read of patient data
  • Accessibility checked (WCAG 2.1 AA) for any screen
  • Product owner accepted it in the sprint review
  • Help text and release note updated




Non-functional requirements
  • Coverage check: under 2 seconds at the 95th percentile
  • Orders are never blocked if a service is down (fail open to the portal)
  • HIPAA minimum necessary: rules read only what the questions need
  • Every decision and every chart query is auditable
  • Decision clocks: 72 hours urgent, 7 calendar days standard
  • No automated denials

How I would run backlog grooming (60 minutes)


  1. Review last sprint's top missing items from the dashboard (5 min)
  2. Walk the next 8–10 stories: does each meet the Definition of Ready? (25 min)
  3. Estimate together; split anything over 8 points (15 min)
  4. Re-rank against WSJF and dependencies; agree the cut line (10 min)
  5. Name impediments and owners (5 min)


What I'd ask in week one: the real first-pass complete rate by service; which EHR partners can launch the questionnaire app today; who signs off clinical rules and how long it takes; and how pend reasons are coded now, so the dashboard can be built from day one.
