Entity resolution · golden profiles · governed overrides

Same company? Prove it.

How a corporate-client master can absorb a vendor refresh without merging two different companies, without undoing what data stewards already fixed, and while explaining every decision to whoever asks.

1 · THE PROBLEMOne company, many records, and two kinds of mistake

A large corporate client shows up in onboarding, credit, markets and payments systems, each with its own spelling, its own copy of the address, and sometimes the wrong identifier. Vendors add more records, some fresher and some older than they look. Joining these into one organisation, with the right parent above it, is what makes a single client view possible.

The two mistakes are not equal. A missed duplicate (one company stored as two) makes the client view incomplete. A false merge (two companies stored as one) is worse: exposure, KYC status and relationship history from one legal entity appear on another, and every system downstream trusts it. So the targets are lopsided on purpose: automatic merges must be very precise, and the system buys back the missed duplicates with targeted human review rather than looser rules.

When a vendor refresh lands, resolve every record to the right legal entity without false merges; keep every steward correction that is still true; re-open, rather than silently apply or drop, any correction that newer evidence contradicts; and be able to explain any match or attribute value to an auditor.

Who depends on the answer

Coverage and relationship teams
Need one view of a client group, including subsidiaries and the sponsor behind a portfolio company.
Onboarding and KYC
Need to know whether a "new" client already exists, before opening a second profile.
Credit and risk
Aggregate exposure up the parent tree. A wrong parent or a false merge moves exposure to the wrong place.
Data stewards
Fix hierarchy defects and duplicates every day. Their fixes are the most expensive data in the system, so losing them is the costliest bug.

2 · THE FICTIONAL WORLDWhat the data looks like, and the traps built into it

Ground truth is known for every record, which is what makes honest scoring possible. In real life you would estimate it from audited samples instead (section 11).

Namesakes

The same brand registered in two countries, and two sponsors whose funds differ only by "Fund III" and "Fund IV". These are different legal entities.

Siblings and parents

"Brand Holdings" and "Brand Logistics" share a name and often a city. Names alone merge them.

Renames and moves

{{ENTITIES}} entities, of which 90 were renamed and 202 moved city in the last year. Older systems still carry the old values.

Wrong identifiers

About 1% of identifiers were copied from a sibling or another company, and some registration numbers have typos.

Legal forms

"Pvt. Ltd.", "P Ltd" and "Private Limited" are the same form. "Private Limited" and "Limited" are not.

Vendor rows for non-clients

Vendors ship companies the bank does not serve. These should not create client profiles.

The rules, and how often each one fired

Rule C4 exists because an earlier version of this pipeline merged two companies in different countries that shared a wrongly copied identifier. A shared ID across countries now always goes to a person. Rule C3 exists for the same reason one level down: same ID, sibling names.

Choosing the auto-merge threshold (held-out pairs)

The model was trained on 40% of entities and tested on pairs from the other 60%, so it never saw the companies it was scored on. Move the threshold to see the trade-off. I picked the lowest threshold that keeps auto-merge precision at or above 99.5%, and let review catch the rest.


Against simpler approaches (same data, full pipeline)

Exact names miss most duplicates. Fuzzy names find them but merge siblings and namesakes ({{B1_FM}} false merges). Identifiers alone still produced {{B2_FM}} false merges, because a wrongly copied ID looks exactly like a right one. The governed pipeline is not clever; it simply refuses to let any single signal decide alone when signals disagree.

How much human review is worth paying for

Widening the review band from {{T_REVIEW}} to 0.3 nearly triples steward hours and recovers only a handful of duplicates. Differences of a few splits between rows are within the noise of the simulated steward (98% accurate, an assumption). I chose {{T_REVIEW}}; the real answer depends on steward capacity and on what a missed duplicate costs each downstream team.

4 · EXPLAINABILITYEvery decision has a reason a person can read

Pick a pair. You get both records, the decision, the rule that made it, and how much each feature pushed the score up or down. This is the view a steward, an auditor or a downstream team should see when they ask "why are these one company?"


5 · GOLDEN PROFILEPicking each attribute, and keeping the receipt

Once records are grouped, each attribute needs one value. I tested three common rules against ground truth: source priority (trust sources in a fixed order), recency (newest value wins), and consensus (most common value wins).

What surprised me. I expected source priority to win legal names. Overall, consensus did ({{NAME_CON}} against {{NAME_PRI}}). But on the {{REN_N}} profiles that belong to recently renamed companies, consensus was right only {{REN_CON}} times, against {{REN_PRI}} for source priority: old systems outvote the fresh registry-style source. So the configured rule for names is consensus, plus a check: when the registry-style vendor disagrees with the majority, a steward looks. That flagged {{HYB_FLAG}} profiles and lifted name accuracy to {{HYB}}. The point is that arbitration rules should be configurable per attribute and tested on data, not chosen by opinion.

Lineage: where each value came from

Each golden value stores the winning record, its source and date, the rule used, and the alternatives it beat.

6 · CHALLENGE AND OVERRIDEThe fix that has to survive the next refresh

Stewards will disagree with the machine, and they will often be right. A good override workflow does four things: it records the change with a reason and evidence; it needs a second person (maker and checker) for anything that changes an entity's identity or hierarchy; it applies the change to every downstream consumer; and it keeps the change alive across re-runs until newer evidence contradicts it, at which point it asks again instead of guessing.

What an override record holds
  • Type: merge, unmerge ("not the same"), or attribute fix
  • Targets by stable record key, not by cluster number (clusters change every run)
  • New value and the value it replaced
  • Reason code and attached evidence (registry extract, annual report)
  • Maker, checker, timestamps
  • Re-open trigger: any source dated after the override that disagrees
The day-30 test

After the first load, stewards made {{N_MERGE_OV}} merges (the remaining duplicates) and {{N_PARENT_OV}} parent fixes, and their {{REVIEW_PAIRS}} review answers were stored. Then a new file from Vendor B arrived, and {{N_ACQ}} companies had been acquired since, {{N_ACQ_OV}} of them among the parent fixes. I re-ran resolution three ways.

Reading it. Stateless is the default when overrides live in a spreadsheet: every run starts over, people re-answer the same questions, and fixes quietly come undone. Sticky is the usual repair: replay every fix forever. It saves the time but turns yesterday's correct parent into today's silent error. Governed replays fixes as constraints and re-opens one only when a newer source disagrees, which here was exactly the {{G_REOPEN}} acquired companies. One merge fix clashed with an earlier steward "not the same" answer; the governed run flags that clash instead of letting either answer win quietly.

7 · THIRD-PARTY DATAEvaluating vendors by what each one is good for

The finding: neither vendor is "the good one". Vendor A has stronger identifiers and parent links that are right {{VA_PAR}} of the time when present, but covers {{VA_COV}} of clients and its values were last checked longer ago (median {{VA_DATE}}). Vendor B covers {{VB_COV}} and is fresher (median {{VB_DATE}}), but its parent links are right only {{VB_PAR}} of the time. So the arbitration rules use A first for parents with B filling gaps, majority vote for identifiers (where A and the internal systems usually agree), and recency for addresses. A vendor contract and feed spec should ask for a date per value, not just a file date: a file dated this month can carry an address last checked two years ago.

How I would onboard a new vendor feed

  1. Map the schema to the existing entity model; add no new attribute without an owner and a consumer.
  2. Normalise legal forms, country codes and identifier formats; reject rows that fail ID format checks.
  3. Shadow run on a full copy: how many rows attach to an existing client, how many conflict with a stored override, how many golden values would change.
  4. Go-live gate: zero new false merges in an audited sample, conflicts with overrides routed to review, and a named owner for the feed.
  5. Keep scoring it monthly on the same scorecard, so renewals are decided on evidence.

8 · DATA QUALITYA short list of measures someone owns

The {{ORPHANS}} parent links that point to no profile are mostly parents that are not clients themselves. That is a product decision, not a data error: create reference-only entities for them, so hierarchies are complete without creating client profiles.

9 · WHERE AI AND AGENTS FITProposals with evidence, decisions by people

Not built in this simulation. These are the two uses I would test first, and how I would judge them before letting either touch production data.

Review packet assistant

For each pair in the review band, draft a one-screen summary: what agrees, what conflicts, what evidence would settle it. It never decides.

  • Measure: steward minutes per pair, with an A/B split of reviewers
  • Guardrail: steward accuracy must not fall on an audited sample
  • Fail if it states a fact that is not in the records
Corporate-action reader

Read filings and news for renames, mergers and acquisitions, and turn each into a proposed override with the source attached. It goes through the same maker-checker flow as a human fix.

  • Measure: share of proposals accepted, time from event to fix
  • Guardrail: zero automatic commits; every proposal links to its source
  • Evaluate on a labelled set of past events before go-live, and keep re-testing

11 · LIMITSWhat this simulation cannot tell me

  • All error rates are invented. Real data has scripts and transliterations, partial ownership, branches and funds with complex structures, and far more volume.
  • Labels here come from ground truth. In practice they come from steward decisions and audited samples, which are themselves imperfect.
  • Steward accuracy (98%) and time per pair (3 minutes) are assumptions.
  • The logistic regression is a stand-in to show how thresholds, explanations and governance fit around a model. A data science team would build and evaluate the real one.
  • I have not worked on entity resolution in production. This page is how I would think about the problem in my first weeks, and what I would ask, not a claim that I have done it.

What I would ask in my first weeks

  • Which downstream consumer hurts most today from a false merge, and which from a duplicate? That sets the thresholds, not a model metric.
  • Where do steward overrides live now, and what happens to them on the next run?
  • Which vendor fields carry a value-level date, and which only a file date?
  • How are sponsor-to-portfolio links and ultimate parents verified today, and how often do they change?
  • What do stewards in the review queue see on screen, and how long does a decision take?
