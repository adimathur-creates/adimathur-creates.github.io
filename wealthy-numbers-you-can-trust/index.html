Product proposal · Client portfolio reporting · written for Wealthy (Holdings, Family Holdings, MF Reports)

Numbers you can trust

An MFD opens a client's report in front of the client. If one number looks wrong, the client stops trusting all of them, and the MFD spends the meeting explaining the app instead of giving advice. This is a check of six ways a mutual fund report can be wrong without anything crashing, how often each shows up in a realistic book, and what I would fix first.

Aditya MathurBengaluruNovember 2026Code and 17 tests

Independent work. Not affiliated with or reviewed by Wealthy, and no Wealthy data. I have not seen how Wealthy's reports compute returns today; the “shortcut report” is a set of common methods, not a description of Wealthy. The book of 2,000 families is simulated and every rate is a labelled assumption in the code. Tax figures are estimates under FY 2026-27 rules, not tax advice.

The short version

  • 80–82% of family reports in the simulated book had at least one number a client could act on wrongly when built with common shortcuts. The rate depends on my assumptions (it ranged 65%–86% across the tests below). How big each error is does not: that part is just maths.
  • Two small display rules clear 60–63% of affected families completely: show absolute return for money held under a year, and say “cost unknown” instead of 0% for holdings bought elsewhere. Neither needs new data.
  • Tax estimates are wrong more often than they are wrong by much. Average-cost shortcuts misclassify short- and long-term gains in 12–14% of families, but the rupee gap is usually small because most gains sit under the ₹1.25 lakh exemption. Worth fixing for ITR season and for CAs, not first.
  • The bigger opportunity is a number reports don't show. 57–66% of members had unused ₹1.25 lakh exemption this year and long-term gains they could book against it. Median room: ₹1,08,431–₹1,25,000, worth up to ₹13,554–₹15,625 of future tax each. That is an MFD conversation, and a reason to open the report before 31 March.

1What a client report has to get right

The JD puts it in two words: accuracy and clarity. In practice that means every number on Holdings, Family Holdings and the MF report must survive three questions from a client sitting next to their MFD: is it right, do I understand it, and what should I do about it? The six problems below fail the first two quietly. Nothing errors out; the number is just wrong or reads wrong.

3The number reports don't show: unused tax-free gains

Each person can book ₹1.25 lakh of long-term gains on equity funds a year without tax. A client who doesn't sell in a year leaves it unused, and it does not carry forward. If they sell units with long-term gains and buy back the same fund, they pay nothing now and their cost goes up, so a later sale is taxed less. In the book:

57–66% of members

had unused exemption this financial year and long-term equity gains they could book against it. ELSS units still in their 3-year lock-in are excluded.

₹1,08,431–₹1,25,000 median room

per member, worth up to ₹13,554–₹15,625 of future tax at 12.5%. In a family of three that can triple.

Why it belongs in reporting: the report already knows each member's realised gains this year and each lot's age. One line, “₹X of gains can still be booked tax-free before 31 March”, gives the MFD a reason to call and something useful to say. Caveats I'd put on the card: it resets the holding period on the repurchased units; the benefit only arrives if those units are sold later; exit loads and small transaction costs apply; and it is a prompt for the MFD, not advice to the client. The exemption is per person and only for equity-oriented funds, not gold or international funds.

4What I would ship, in order

  1. Two display rules (week 1–2). Absolute return under one year, XIRR after. “Cost unknown” instead of 0% for holdings without purchase history, with the family return labelled “covers X% of value”.
  2. Correct family maths (week 2–4). Pooled family XIRR. Switch-out and switch-in linked as internal transfers. Total invested counts only money that came from outside.
  3. Checks before a number is shown (week 3–6). Units per folio reconciled against registrar records. Each check either passes, labels the number, or holds it with a reason the MFD can read out. A held number is better than a wrong one, but every held line is a cost, so the held-back rate is a tracked metric with a ceiling.
  4. Fill the gaps (month 2). Ask for the client's detailed CAS at onboarding, since it carries the transactions needed for cost and XIRR. This is what lifts coverage above 83%.
  5. Tax P&L and the exemption card (month 2–3). FIFO lots, per-lot holding periods, grandfathering, debt bought after April 2023 at slab. Then the unused-exemption line, timed for January to March.

How I'd know it worked

  • Share of report lines that pass every check (target set after a baseline week)
  • Share of family value with known cost
  • Support tickets that say a return or total “looks wrong”, per 1,000 reports opened
  • Reports shared by MFDs with clients, per active MFD

Guardrails

  • Held-back lines stay under a set share of value; if they rise, fix data before adding checks
  • Every held line shows a reason and a next step
  • Recompute nightly and diff against yesterday; large unexplained moves block the report for that client

6What this does not show

  • How Wealthy's reports actually work today. Some of these may already be solved. The point is the checklist and the priority order, which I'd run against real reports in week one.
  • Stocks, bonds, PMS, AIFs and insurance. Multi-asset reports add corporate actions, accrued interest and valuation lag. Same idea, more checks.
  • Dividends (IDCW), stamp duty, exit loads, surcharge and cess, and slab rates for debt gains.
  • Monthly NAVs. Real XIRR uses daily NAVs and exact dates.
  • Whether clients would act on the exemption card. That needs a test with a group of MFDs.

7Method and tests

XIRR is solved by bisection and checked against Excel's documented example (37.34%). Tax follows FY 2026-27 rules: equity-oriented funds 20% short-term (held 12 months or less), 12.5% long-term above ₹1.25 lakh a year per person, no indexation; cost for units bought before 1 Feb 2018 is the higher of actual cost and the lower of the 31 Jan 2018 value and sale value; debt funds bought from 1 Apr 2023 at slab rate whatever the holding period. A test reproduces the public worked example: ₹1.8 lakh of long-term gains → ₹6,875 tax. Other tests: FIFO order, grandfathering both ways, SIP units sold within a year counted as short-term, pooled vs averaged family XIRR, switches as internal transfers, ELSS lock-in, held-back holdings never shown as 0%, and determinism by seed. Run node test/calc.test.js and node test/report.test.js.
