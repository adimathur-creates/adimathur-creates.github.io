The Slow Scam

Google's newest India scam protections step in while a scam is happening live on a phone call. But most money is lost to investment scams that run for days, with no call at the moment of payment. This is how I would catch those on Google Pay, before the big transfer.

By Aditya MathurLinkedInCode, data and tests

Independent work. I don't work at Google, this is not a Google product, and I have no access to Google data. Facts about scams and Google's features come from public sources (listed at the end). Every result marked simulated comes from a synthetic population I built. It shows the method and the trade-offs; it is not a forecast.

In one minute

  • The problem. Indians reported losing {{lossCr}} to cyber fraud in 2025, and about three-quarters of it went to fake investment schemes. These run for days in WhatsApp and Telegram groups. The victim pays on their own, to a different stranger's UPI ID each time, often after a small "profit" is paid to them first.
  • The gap. Google's 2025 protections for India look for a live scam: an unknown caller, screen sharing, a payment app opened mid-call. A slow investment scam trips none of these. Fraud lists catch the receiving IDs, but scammers change them about daily.
  • The bet. The receiving ID changes. The victim's pattern doesn't. Score each payment on the payer's own history (new people, rising amounts, a stranger paid you first), then respond in steps: a one-tap question, a warning that names the scam, a short hold.
  • Simulated result. In the median journey, {{jl_ahead}}% of the money is still in the victim's account when the first full warning appears.
  • What I would do first at Google. Replay the score on six months of real Google Pay history with confirmed scam labels, before showing anything to anyone. The plan below says how, and what would make me kill it.

1The problem, in numbers

  • Big and still rising in count. India's cybercrime reporting portal recorded {{lossCr}} lost and about 21.8 lakh complaints in 2025.
  • Mostly investment scams. Moneylife puts 76% of 2025 losses on fake investment schemes; I4C's breakdown has investment schemes at 77% and digital arrest at 8%. So the scam that gets the headlines is not where most of the money goes.
  • "UPI fraud" counts miss most of it. The Finance Ministry told Parliament that UPI fraud cost {{upiFraud}} from January to November 2025. That is a small slice of the total, because the victim authorises every payment themselves. To the system, it looks like a normal payment.
  • Recovery is poor. Moneylife reports that about three out of four rupees lost are never recovered. The money moves through chains of mule accounts within hours.

How the scam runs, from the victim's side

Bank and RBI advisories describe the same pattern again and again:

  1. The hook. An ad, an SMS or a WhatsApp/Telegram group offers easy tasks ("like these videos") or stock tips with big returns.
  2. The bait. The victim is paid a small amount first: a task "commission" or a "profit withdrawal". This builds trust. Bank advisories tell people to check where that first commission came from.
  3. The deposits. The victim is told to pay to unlock bigger tasks or to invest. Each payment goes to a personal UPI ID, often a different one each time. The RBI advisory notes these UPI details are updated daily.
  4. The trap. The fake app shows growing profits. To withdraw, the victim must pay a "tax", a "fee" or a "margin call". Each payment is bigger than the last.
  5. The end. Savings are gone. Some victims move to bank transfers once UPI limits get in the way. That money is outside any UPI app's view.

3The insight: the receiving ID changes, the victim's pattern doesn't

A payment app sees the payer's own history. That history is very different during a scam:

What a scam looks like from the payer's side

  • Several people paid for the first time in a few days
  • A small credit from a stranger, then payments to other strangers
  • Each payment bigger than the last, across different people
  • Total paid to new people far above the person's usual month
  • Personal UPI IDs, never an @valid one, for something called an "investment"

The people a bad solution would hurt

Many honest payments look like this too. I built these people into the simulation on purpose, and the results report their friction separately:

  • Someone moving flat: a token, a big deposit and a broker, all to people they have never paid.
  • A shopkeeper on a personal UPI ID: paid by many strangers every day, trying new suppliers.
  • A freelancer: paid by new clients, pays new helpers.
  • A student: new friends send money for splits; pays people they just met.
  • Someone buying second-hand: one big payment to a stranger on OLX.
  • A real investor: big, rising payments, but to @valid broker and fund IDs.

4The design

A journey score for each payment to a person

Before a payment to a personal UPI ID goes out, add up these signals. Payments to merchants and to @valid IDs are not scored. Payments under ₹500 never get friction. People you have paid and been paid by before are not scored.

Signal (what the user would be told)PointsWhy
First payment to this person1.0Every scam payment is to someone new.
New people paid in 7 days (2 / 3–4 / 5+), ignoring tiny ones like an auto ride0.6 / 1.4 / 2.0Receiving IDs rotate, so victims pay many new people fast.
A stranger sent you a small amount (up to ₹5,000) in the last 10 days, and now you pay a different stranger at least 1.5x that1.6The bait. Cut to a quarter for people paid by 8+ strangers a month, like shops.
This payment is 1.8x / 3x the biggest recent payment to a different new person0.9 / 1.3Escalation across people. A token then a deposit to the same landlord does not count.
Money to new people in 14 days is 0.5x / 1.5x your usual month0.7 / 1.4Scaled to each person, so it works for a student and a business owner.
This UPI ID was paid for the first time by 5+ / 15+ people today0.8 / 1.3Mule IDs collect from many victims. Weak alone: busy shops look the same.
This UPI ID is on a government fraud list3.0Precise but late. Adds to the score instead of acting alone.
Round amount of ₹5,000 or more0.2A small nudge.

The weights are hand-set and readable on purpose. At Google I would start here as a baseline and compare it with a model trained on real labels. A score people can read is easier to defend to Legal, to users and to regulators.

A ladder of responses, not one big warning

UK research on these "authorised" scams found that generic warnings barely work: people stop reading them, and scammers coach victims to click through. What worked in a controlled study was a warning shown only for risky payments, with a clear button to cancel or wait. That cut fraud by 81% in the study, against 18% for a general warning that also lost force with repeat exposure. So the design keeps friction rare and makes each step specific.

0 · NothingScore under {{t1}}The payment goes through as today. This is almost every payment.
1 · Quick questionScore {{t1}}+"What's this payment for?" One tap. If the answer is "investment" or "a job or task", go straight to step 2. Asked once per person.
2 · Warning that names the scamScore {{t2}}+Says why in plain words ("a stranger paid you ₹300, now you're paying a different stranger"). Main button: don't pay now. "Pay anyway" unlocks after 30 seconds.
3 · Short holdScore {{t3}}+The payment waits 4 hours unless cancelled. One tap to call 1930 or someone they trust. Needs Legal and NPCI sign-off first.

Fatigue rules: one full warning per 72 hours unless the risk rises by a full point; one hold per 24 hours. Scammers will coach victims to answer "family" to the question, so later steps depend on the score, not only on the answer.

What the results say

  • Fraud lists alone are precise but late. They reach {{li_reached}}% of victims, often after the money has moved, and protect {{li_protected}}%.
  • An amount rule reaches people, but buries everyone in warnings. {{am_users}}% of genuine users would see a full warning every month. People learn to ignore that.
  • The journey score gets more protection with far less noise, and adding the lists as one signal improves it ({{jo_protected}}% → {{jl_protected}}%).
  • It acts early. {{jl_by3}}% of victims see a full warning by their third scam payment. In the median journey, {{jl_ahead}}% of the money is still theirs at that point.
  • Its blind spot: quick scams under ₹500 a payment. The "no friction under ₹500" rule lets them through (reached {{quick_reached}}% vs 100% for task and stock scams). I think that trade is right: those losses are small and friction on every small payment would hurt millions.

7How I would launch it and test it

Phases

  1. Replay (weeks 1–4). Run the score over six months of Google Pay history and join it to confirmed scam reports (1930 and portal reports matched to transactions, user reports, bank disputes). Draw the real version of the trade-off chart. If the curve isn't clearly better than today's warnings, stop here.
  2. Shadow (2 weeks). Score live payments without showing anything. Check speed, coverage and how many people would be asked.
  3. Question only, 1% of users. Learn how people answer, how many drop off, and how often later-confirmed victims answered "family".
  4. Full warnings. Test with two designs at once (below). Rewrite the copy until people actually stop.
  5. Holds. Only after Legal and NPCI agree. Start with people who switch it on themselves, for example older users or families protecting a parent.
  6. Scale. All languages, voice read-out, and share the journey signal with RBI's planned intelligence platform so that switching apps doesn't beat it.

The experiment

  • Primary metric: confirmed scam loss per 1,000 users per month.
  • Why it's hard: scams are rare. To detect a 20% drop in the share of users scammed, with 80% power, you need about {{n1k}} users per group if 1 in 1,000 users is scammed in a month, and about {{n10k}} if it's 1 in 10,000. Google Pay has the scale; a small team doesn't.
  • Design 1, regression discontinuity: compare payments just above and just below the warning threshold. Nobody at high risk is denied protection, which matters ethically.
  • Design 2, a small holdout for the new steps only: today's protections stay on for everyone. Keep it short and stop early if harm shows.
  • Leading signal: after a full warning, did the person stop paying new people for 7 days? This arrives in days, not months.

Metrics

Goal and drivers

  • Goal: ₹ lost to scams per 1,000 active users a month (confirmed)
  • Share of confirmed scam journeys with a full warning by the 3rd payment
  • Share who stop paying new people within 48 hours of a warning
  • Time from scam to report (faster reports freeze more money)

Guardrails

  • Full warnings per 1,000 genuine users a month: budget ≤ 6
  • Payments completed after the question: ≥ 97%
  • Support contacts about stopped payments
  • Users leaving for another app after a warning
  • Friction by group, state and language
When I would stop or rethink: fewer than 15% stop after a full warning after 4 weeks of copy changes; more than 10 full warnings per 1,000 genuine users a month at the launch threshold; payments to people drop by more than 2% for scored users; or the replay curve is not clearly better than today's warnings.

9Risks, and what I can't know from outside

Risks

  • Coached victims. Scammers already tell people what to tap. That is why the score, not the answer, drives later steps.
  • Moving to another app. A scammer can say "use a different app". A warning still plants doubt, but the real fix is sharing the signal across the industry.
  • Unfair friction. Freelancers, shopkeepers and students get more of it. Each needs its own budget.
  • Holding money. Delaying a payment someone chose to make is a serious step. It needs a clear legal basis and an easy way out.
  • Scams change. Scripts adapt. The score needs regular checks against fresh labels and someone actively trying to beat it.
  • My numbers are simulated. The direction should hold. The size of the gain must come from real data.

What I'd ask in my first week

  • What triggers today's million-plus weekly warnings, and how often do people stop after one?
  • How much investment-scam money leaves through Google Pay, compared with net banking?
  • How good are the scam labels, and how long until a scam is reported?
  • Is there already a count of first-time payers per receiving ID?

All code is in the repository:

Main assumptions in the simulation
  • Victim share oversampled to {{victimRate}} so there are enough journeys to measure. Precision is reported at realistic rates instead.
  • Task scams: 1–3 bait credits of ₹100–800, then 4–8 payments starting at ₹800–2,500 and growing 1.6–3x each time. Stock-tips scams: first deposit ₹5,000–25,000, a ₹1,000–5,000 "profit" a few days later, then 4–11 deposits growing 1.2–2.2x, capped at ₹1 lakh each; half later move money by bank transfer (1–4x more). Quick scams: 2–5 payments from ₹199.
  • Receiving IDs: a new one 75% of the time. They are about a day old at most; the chance one is already on a fraud list grows with age (half-life about 2 days).
  • Innocent IDs wrongly on a list: 0.05% of payments to people.
  • Responses: 3% stop after the question only (when answered falsely); 35% after a full warning, 70% as effective each repeat; 60% after a hold. 60% of victims answer the question honestly. 3% of genuine users answer "investment" for a payment to a person. Genuine users abandon 8% of payments after a full warning, 15% after a hold.
