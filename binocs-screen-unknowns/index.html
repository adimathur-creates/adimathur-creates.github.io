Product proposal · Deal Screening · written for Binocs

The deal you never saw

Teasers leave numbers out. A screening tool that must say yes or no will quietly reject deals that fit, and nobody finds out. A decision layer that asks the banker one or two questions first keeps those deals and still saves analyst time.

Aditya MathurBengaluruNovember 2026Code and 16 tests

Independent work, not affiliated with or reviewed by Binocs. Teasers, companies and the mandate are fictional. The simulation uses labelled assumptions you can change in the code. It does not test Binocs' extraction or any real customer data.

The short version

  • The problem. Binocs' Deal Screening promises "a clear, defensible verdict" with "no grey areas" [source]. But early screens run on incomplete data [source]. When a teaser doesn't state EBITDA or the stake for sale, a forced verdict has to guess.
  • Why it matters. A wrong "yes" costs an associate about 5 hours and gets noticed. A wrong "no" costs a deal that fit, and nobody ever notices. A tool tuned on visible errors drifts toward rejecting too much.
  • The result. In 30 simulated years of 800 inbound deals, a forced yes/no kept only 34% of the deals that truly fit the mandate (range 4% to 45%). Asking up to two banker questions first kept all of them. It also ran 67% fewer wasted full screens than passing everything not ruled out, and had the lowest cost per good deal found (11.1 hours vs 12.9 and 16.4).
  • The proposal. Add a third verdict, Ask: the exact question to send, ranked by how likely the answer settles the deal, at most two per deal, drafted as one email. Keep the verdict clear for the partner, and be honest about what is not known.

1The problem, from the user's side

Associate

Reads 15–20 teasers a week. A first screen takes 4–6 hours per CIM [source]. Wants the tool to throw out obvious misses and never throw out a real one.

Partner

Wants a verdict and a one-pager they can trust in 2 minutes. Loses trust the first time they hear a competitor bought a deal the tool rejected.

Banker (sell side)

Expects a buyer to respond fast. Will answer a sharp question ("is a majority stake available?"). Ignores a 12-question list.

The gap. Most teasers lead with sector and geography and hold back the rest until an NDA. In my assumed disclosure rates, only 70% of teasers state EBITDA, 55% say whether a majority stake is on offer, and 30% mention customer concentration. A tool with only "pass" or "reject" has to treat each blank as one or the other. Both are wrong a lot of the time.

Where the cost goes

  • Banker questions: about 198 a year across 104 deals. That's 1–2 a week, sent as one email per deal.
  • Unanswered: 45 deals a year still have a gap after the questions (assuming 75% of questions get answered). A person makes the call on those, so a slow banker costs time, not deals.
  • Banker reply rate: at 30% replies the human time rises to 533 hours. That's still below the 595 hours of passing everything, and no fitting deal is lost.

4The feature spec

Verdicts

VerdictWhenWhat the user sees
PassEvery criterion is stated and inside the mandateVerdict, evidence line per criterion with the quote it came from, and the IC one-pager
RejectAny stated criterion is outside the mandateThe one criterion that failed and its quote. A stated failure always beats a missing field: never ask about a deal that's already out.
Ask (new)Nothing stated fails, but a criterion is not in the teaserUp to 2 questions, ranked by how likely the answer settles the deal, pre-written as one email to the banker. Re-screens when the reply is pasted or forwarded.
Review (new)Near miss: EBITDA within 15% under the floor and growing 15%+ a yearRouted to a person with the reason. These are the deals a partner is angriest to lose.

Rules

  1. Question order: ask first about the criterion that most often fails (EBITDA, then stake, then growth), so the most deals settle with one answer. Learn the rates per customer from their own past pipeline.
  2. Cap at 2 questions per deal. More than that reads like a checklist to the banker, and response rates drop.
  3. Deal-breakers vs quality screens. The fund marks each criterion. Only a deal-breaker can block a pass on its own.
  4. Every verdict is reconstructable from the evidence table alone: field, value, quote, rule. No score without a reason.
  5. The one-pager shows an "Unknowns" box, so the partner sees what the verdict is built on.

Edge cases I would test first

  • Ranges ("EBITDA of $8–10M"): pass only if the whole range fits, otherwise Ask.
  • Adjusted vs reported EBITDA, LTM vs fiscal year, and currency (Project Maple reports in C$). Normalise before the rule runs, and show the conversion.
  • Teaser and CIM disagree: the later document wins, and the change is flagged on the deal.
  • Banker replies "available on request after NDA": counts as unanswered, not as a no.
  • The mandate changes mid-year: re-screen open Ask and Review deals, never closed ones.

Rollout

  1. Shadow (2 weeks): run on 2 design-partner funds' past pipeline. Report lost fitting deals under the current verdict vs Ask.
  2. Opt-in: the Ask verdict shows to associates, and emails are drafted, not sent.
  3. Default on once recall and override targets hold for a month.

Questions I would bring to the founder

  • What share of current screening verdicts are made with a deal-breaker missing? That one log query would size the problem in a day.
  • Do customers already override verdicts, and is the override captured? That is the labelled data this needs.
  • Is the IC one-pager read before or after the verdict? That changes where the Unknowns box goes.

5Method and limits

  • Assumes extraction is correct. Binocs' extraction quality is not tested here; a wrong extracted number is a separate failure mode.
  • Disclosure rates, banker reply rate and most hours are my assumptions, labelled in src/simulate.js. The direction holds as they change (see the chart and tests). The exact numbers do not.
  • "Truly fits" means it meets every criterion on full information. It is not "a good investment". Screening only decides who gets a closer look.
  • Deterministic rules, no model calls. That is deliberate: a verdict a partner can defend has to be reproducible.
