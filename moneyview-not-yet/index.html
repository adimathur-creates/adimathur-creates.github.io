Not yet, not no

Many people who apply for a loan on a lending app are declined. What they see next decides whether they come back eligible in three months, or keep re-applying, lower their score and go to a costlier lender. This proposal turns a decline into a specific path back, without lowering credit quality.

Aditya MathurBengaluruNovember 2026Code and 9 tests

Independent work. Not affiliated with or reviewed by Moneyview, and no Moneyview data. I haven't seen Moneyview's actual decline screens; I didn't apply for a loan to look, because that would add a hard enquiry to my credit report. The decline reasons, their shares and every behaviour rate are labelled assumptions in the code. The numbers show the shape of the trade-off, not Moneyview's results.

The short version

  • The gap. Moneyview had 140.28 million registered users and 11.9 million monetised users at June 2026, and processed more than 200,000 loan applications a day [source]. Most registered users have never taken a loan, and every declined applicant joins that group.
  • The rule already exists. RBI's fair practices code requires lenders to tell applicants in writing the main reasons their application was rejected [source]. A common pattern across lending apps is a reason plus "you can reapply after 30 days" [source]. That meets the rule but doesn't help anyone come back.
  • The proposal. A "not yet" path: the reason in plain words (in Hindi too), the one change that would help, a date when it's worth checking, a soft pre-check that doesn't touch the credit score, an immediate smaller-amount offer where affordability was the problem, and an honest "we can't" with alternatives when nothing will change.
  • The result (simulated, 60,000 declined users, 26 weeks, 5 runs). Even assuming a plan changes nobody's behaviour, re-approvals went from 177 to 425 per 1,000 declined users, hard enquiries per user fell by 42%, and users caught in a loop of three or more declines fell from 34% to 4%.
  • The cost, stated plainly. Re-approved users default more: 4.08% against 3.50%, mainly from temporary fixes and smaller-amount offers. The extra approvals default at about 4.5%, well under a break-even of 9.1% on my assumptions. That's a credit-team decision, and the A/B test below is designed to measure it.

1Why this is worth a PM's time

What Moneyview has

  • Scale: 140.28 million registered users, 11.9 million monetised, 200,000+ applications a day [source].
  • Growth is coming from repeat borrowers: a rising share of the loan book is second and later loans [source]. A declined user who comes back eligible is the next repeat borrower.
  • A free credit tracker already in the app [source]: the natural home for a soft pre-check and a check date.

Why it can't just approve more

  • Credit cost is the constraint. Impairment was 5.16% of average managed AUM in FY2026, and Stage 3 loans at Whizdm Finance were 2.72% in June 2026 [source].
  • So this can't be "lower the bar". It has to be "help the right people clear it": those whose reason is fixable or temporary, confirmed on their credit report, not self-declared.
  • Every blind re-application is a hard enquiry, and too many enquiries is itself a decline reason. The status quo pushes some users into that loop.

Problem statement. After a decline, (1) bring back applicants whose reason is fixable or temporary, once they're actually eligible; (2) stop applicants from damaging their own credit by re-applying blindly; (3) don't raise the default rate of re-approved loans past what the credit team accepts.

Where the gain comes from

  • Timing. People apply when the pre-check says yes, not on hope. Fewer wasted enquiries, fewer spirals.
  • Quiet periods. For "too many enquiries", the fix is to stop applying. Blind re-application resets the clock every time.
  • The smaller amount. 59 per 1,000 take a smaller loan on the spot instead of leaving.

Where it costs

  • Some guided fixes are temporary: a card balance paid down, then run up again. I assumed 20%, at twice the default rate.
  • Smaller-amount loans to stretched borrowers default more (assumed 1.25x).
  • The fix: re-approve only on a fix confirmed on the bureau report, and cap the smaller amount by EMI-to-income.

Is the extra risk worth it?

Assume each repaid loan earns about ₹4,000 over its life and each default loses about ₹40,000 (my assumptions; the credit team has the real numbers). Then extra approvals pay off as long as they default below C ÷ (C + L) = 9.1%.

4What I'd ship first, and how I'd test it

v1 scope (one sprint of design, two of build)

  1. Map each lender decline code to one user-facing reason, one action and one check date. Write it in English and Hindi first.
  2. Smaller-amount offer at decline for affordability declines, within the same EMI-to-income limit.
  3. A check date in the credit tracker, with a soft pre-check and a reminder.
  4. For policy declines: say it plainly, and show alternatives that fit (for example a secured card), not another loan.

Guardrails

  • Re-approval only on a fix confirmed on the bureau report, never on the user's word.
  • No promises: "worth checking", never "you'll be approved".
  • Reason text reviewed with the lending partner, since the lender owns the decline.
  • No more than one reminder a week, and an opt-out.

Questions I'd bring to the team

  • What share of declines do partner lenders return with a usable reason code, and how granular is it?
  • How many declined users re-apply within 30 days today, and how often are they declined again?
  • Does the credit tracker already use soft pulls that could power a pre-check?

5Method and limits

  • Weekly agent simulation (src/sim.js) of 60,000 declined users over 26 weeks. Each user has one decline reason with its own way of getting fixed. Status quo users re-apply on hope (6% a week after the 30-day block); guided users apply mostly once they're eligible.
  • The reason mix, fix rates and default multiples are assumptions, not data. The single biggest unknown, how much a plan changes behaviour, is shown at zero, half and full effect.
  • Not modelled: users who were declined by one partner lender but would be approved by another; the soft pre-check's own error rate; the cost of reminders; fraud.
  • The decline screens are my mock-ups. The Hindi copy should be reviewed by a native writer and by the lending partner before use.
